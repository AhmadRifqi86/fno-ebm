\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Evidential Methods for Neural Operators}

Our experiments establish evidential deep learning as the preferred approach for uncertainty quantification in 
neural operators. Key advantages include:

\textbf{Superior calibration}: Evidential methods (ECE $\sim$ 0.004) outperform MC Dropout (0.019) and Deep 
Ensembles (0.011) while maintaining 93--95\% coverage. This superiority holds across both Navier-Stokes and 
Darcy Flow, with the performance gap widening on harder problems.

\textbf{Computational efficiency}: Single-pass inference provides 5--30$\times$ speedup over alternatives, 
critical for real-time applications and high-throughput scientific computing workflows
\cite{karniadakis2021physics,raissi2019physics}.

\textbf{Informative uncertainty}: Uncertainty-error correlation of 0.61 (vs. 0.31--0.33 for baselines) indicates 
evidential uncertainty better reflects prediction quality.

\textbf{MC Dropout unsuitable}: Across all metrics, MC Dropout exhibits inferior performance 
(6$\times$ worse MSE, lowest correlation), confirming it should be avoided for neural operators. 
The spatial coherence of PDE solutions may be incompatible with dropout's stochastic regularization.

\subsection{Physical Consistency of Predicted Uncertainty}

For a surrogate deployed in scientific computing, the operationally relevant question is not whether
uncertainty correlates with error in aggregate, but whether it localizes where the prediction violates the
governing equations. Experiment 3 measures this directly through the alignment between predicted uncertainty
and the pointwise PDE residual, and the answer is conditional rather than uniform. On the transient
Navier-Stokes problem the alignment is real: rank correlations of $0.43$--$0.63$ and concentration ratios of
$1.5$--$1.6$ mean the model's most uncertain regions are also, by a clear margin, the ones that most violate
the discrete vorticity balance. On the smooth steady Darcy problem the same measurement returns essentially
zero, because the model solves that problem almost exactly and near-homoscedastically, leaving no
heterogeneous residual field for the uncertainty to track. The lesson for practitioners is that physical
consistency of the predicted uncertainty is an \emph{emergent} property of hard, spatially heterogeneous
problems, not a guarantee of the evidential parameterization---it should be verified per problem, not
assumed. We also note a methodological caveat: on Navier-Stokes the Pearson correlation is much weaker than
the Spearman value ($0.06$--$0.20$ vs.\ $0.43$--$0.63$), because the strong-form residual on time-resolved
turbulence carries a large irreducible floor; rank-based and concentration measures are the more reliable
diagnostics of physical consistency in that regime.

\textbf{Practical implication}: Practitioners should treat evidential outputs as \emph{total uncertainty}
rather than interpreting the epistemic-aleatoric decomposition, which the NIG parameterization does not
constrain independently. For applications requiring true epistemic-aleatoric separation, Deep Ensembles
remain preferable despite computational cost.

\subsection{Reinterpreting OOD Detection}

The stark contrast between parameter-level OOD (AUROC = 0.55) and operator-level OOD (AUROC = 1.0) suggests a 
domain-specific interpretation: parameter extrapolation within the same PDE family may not constitute true 
distributional shift from the operator learning perspective.

The FNO learns the Navier-Stokes operator in Fourier space \cite{li2020fourier,kovachki2023neural}, which
generalizes across Reynolds numbers despite different flow characteristics. This explains maintained confidence at Re = 5000. Conversely, cross-PDE transfer 
represents genuine operator-level shift where the governing equations differ fundamentally, yielding appropriate 
massive uncertainty increases (36--378$\times$).

This finding has deployment implications: evidential uncertainty reliably monitors operator-level distributional
shift but should not be solely relied upon for parameter extrapolation validation.

\subsection{Uncertainty Under Harder Regimes: Shocks and Rollout}

Experiments 5 and 6 subject the predicted uncertainty to two regimes beyond the smooth benchmarks, and together
they expose a single governing mechanism. On shock-capturing compressible Euler flow (Experiment 5), uncertainty
succeeds emphatically: it concentrates $3.9\times$ on the steepest density gradients and correlates with
$|\nabla\rho|$ at $0.62$--$0.66$ across schemes, because discontinuities are exactly where the solution map is
hardest and the model most error-prone---local difficulty and local error coincide. Under long-horizon
autoregressive rollout (Experiment 6), uncertainty fails just as emphatically: it collapses to under a fifth of
its initial value while error grows, anti-correlated at $-0.94$.

The two outcomes are not in tension; they are the same property viewed from two sides. The evidential head emits
uncertainty as a deterministic, pointwise function of the \emph{current input's local structure}, and that
function is calibrated only on the training distribution. Where hardness is legible in the local input---a sharp
gradient---the uncertainty is correct. But autoregressive rollout drives the state through the FNO's spectral
low-pass repeatedly, producing progressively smoother fields that are increasingly wrong; the model reads these
blurred inputs as easy and grows confident precisely as it drifts off the training manifold. This is not a
conjecture: the predicted uncertainty tracks the field roughness at a mean correlation of $0.99$ across all six
schemes (Fig.~\ref{fig:rollout}), so it is effectively a readout of local input smoothness, blind to the
accumulated error. This is the same
over-confidence-under-shift seen for parameter extrapolation in Experiment~4, now resolved temporally. The
practical implication is sharp: evidential uncertainty is trustworthy as a within-distribution difficulty
detector but must not be used as a rollout stopping criterion or an out-of-distribution alarm, because it moves
the wrong way exactly when a practitioner would rely on it most.

Crucially, neither face of this behavior is an artifact of the evidential parameterization. Running MC Dropout and
Deep Ensemble through the identical protocol reproduces both signs: the sampling-based methods concentrate
uncertainty on shocks ($3.6$--$3.7\times$, versus $3.9\times$ for evidential) and collapse identically under
rollout (uncertainty shrinks to $9$--$17\%$ of its initial value with step correlation $\approx -0.91$, versus
$-0.94$ for evidential), with their uncertainty tracking input roughness at correlation $0.98$. This is the more
consequential observation: the pathology is not that evidence is a poor uncertainty surrogate, but that
\emph{pointwise, in-distribution-calibrated} uncertainty---whether parameterized as evidence or estimated from
model disagreement---cannot by construction sense global drift off the training manifold. Deep Ensembles, often
recommended precisely for epistemic robustness, fail here for the same structural reason, because each member
reads the same smoothed rollout state as easy.

\subsection{Architectural Insights}

Our ablation study identifies critical components for evidential FNOs. Skip connections
\cite{he2016deep} and GELU activations \cite{hendrycks2016gaussian} prove essential---their removal causes
catastrophic failure with MSE approaching 1.0 and undefined correlation metrics. 
Global residual connections provide moderate benefits with approximately 5\% improvement in calibration quality. 
Conversely, batch normalization \cite{ioffe2015batch} proves detrimental, yielding 2.2$\times$ worse performance,
likely due to interference with the NIG parameter learning dynamics where the normalization disrupts the delicate balance 
between $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\nu$ outputs. Notably, evidential head complexity matters little; shallow heads 
maintain prediction quality with fewer parameters, suggesting the uncertainty estimation burden falls primarily on 
the FNO backbone rather than the output projection.

While the Fourier layers provide the global receptive field necessary for operator learning, the ablation results 
in Table VII demonstrate that they are architecturally dependent on spatial skip connections for numerical 
stability. The collapse of the fourier\_only variant does not imply a lack of contribution from the 
spectral branch; rather, it underscores that the spectral-spatial duality is essential for the stable 
parameterization of the Normal-Inverse-Gamma distribution in evidential frameworks.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Pointwise independence assumption}: The most consequential limitation is structural. Applying the NIG
parameterization independently at each grid point assumes that predictive uncertainties are conditionally
independent across space. This is physically implausible for PDE solutions, whose errors exhibit strong spatial
correlation inherited from the continuity of the underlying field and from the FNO's spectral truncation, which
discards high-frequency modes globally rather than locally. Consequently, the per-point marginals we report are
well-defined, but the implied joint distribution over the field is not: functionals that integrate over the
domain---total mass, kinetic energy, flux through a boundary---will have their uncertainty systematically
underestimated, since independent errors average out at a rate that correlated errors do not. Addressing this
requires modeling spatial covariance directly, for instance by placing evidence on spectral coefficients rather
than grid points, or by fusing evidence across scales. We regard this as the principal direction for future work.

Further limitations: (1) the core calibration and physical-consistency results are established on two PDEs
(Navier-Stokes, Darcy Flow); although we additionally probe shock-dominated flow (Experiment~5) and long
autoregressive rollouts (Experiment~6), behavior on non-uniform grids, complex geometries, and 3D problems
remains untested; (2) single architecture (FNO) tested---other operators may behave differently; (3) synthetic
training data may not capture real-world measurement noise; (4) limited OOD scenarios tested; (5) Experiment~6
characterizes uncertainty drift over rollout and finds it anti-correlated with error, but we do not yet propose
a remedy---a rollout-aware or distribution-shift-sensitive evidence mechanism is left to future work.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents a comprehensive comparison of uncertainty quantification methods for Fourier Neural Operators, 
evaluating Monte Carlo Dropout, Deep Ensembles, and Evidential Deep Learning across calibration quality,
computational efficiency, physical consistency, and out-of-distribution detection. Our experiments demonstrate
that evidential methods achieve state-of-the-art calibration with Expected Calibration Error around 0.004 using
single-pass inference, outperforming both Monte Carlo Dropout and Deep Ensembles by significant margins while
providing 5--30 times faster inference. We further find that out-of-distribution detection capability depends
critically on the type of distributional shift---parameter extrapolation yields near-random detection while
operator-level shifts achieve perfect separation, suggesting that neural operators learn physics-informed
representations that generalize within equation families. Stress-testing on harder regimes sharpens the picture:
the predicted uncertainty concentrates strongly on shocks yet collapses under long autoregressive rollout, two
faces of one mechanism---a pointwise, locally-calibrated evidence that detects hard inputs but not global
departure from the training distribution.

Based on these findings, we recommend using evidential FNO with Inverse-Variance or Log-Barrier regularization for 
production deployment, reporting total uncertainty rather than relying on the epistemic-aleatoric decomposition,
and reserving Deep Ensembles for applications requiring robust epistemic uncertainty quantification. Monte Carlo
Dropout should be avoided for neural operators due to consistently poor performance. The central open problem is
the pointwise independence assumption underlying per-grid-point evidential parameterization: future work should
investigate spatially correlated evidence---for instance evidence placed on spectral coefficients, or
multi-scale evidence fusion---and extend evaluation to broader partial differential equation families,
non-uniform discretizations, and neural operator architectures.
