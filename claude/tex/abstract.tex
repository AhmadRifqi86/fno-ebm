\begin{abstract}

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) is essential for reliable deployment of neural operators in scientific computing and engineering applications. While Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) have demonstrated remarkable success in solving partial differential equations (PDEs), their deterministic nature limits their ability to provide trustworthy uncertainty estimates. This paper presents a systematic comparison of three prominent uncertainty quantification approaches for neural operators: Monte Carlo Dropout, Deep Ensembles, and Evidential Deep Learning, with a focus on in-distribution calibration and prediction interval quality.

We implement evidential neural operator architectures that output Normal-Inverse-Gamma (NIG) distributional parameters in a single forward pass, providing computational efficiency advantages over sampling-based methods. Our comprehensive evaluation across four benchmark PDEs (Burgers equation, advection equation, diffusion-reaction systems, and Navier-Stokes equations) assesses calibration quality (Expected Calibration Error), coverage guarantees, predictive performance (MSE, MAE, relative $L^2$ error), and uncertainty-error correlation. Through systematic ablation studies, we investigate the impact of six regularization schemes on evidential learning: standard, improved, uncertainty-aware, annealed, $L^2$ evidence, and KL-divergence regularization.

Our results demonstrate that evidential methods with improved regularization achieve excellent calibration (ECE $\sim$ 0.003) and near-nominal coverage ($\sim$ 93\%) while maintaining competitive predictive accuracy (MSE $\sim$ 0.0016). Evidential methods provide these benefits with significantly lower computational cost (single model, single forward pass) compared to ensembles (5-10$\times$ overhead). However, through systematic evaluation, we identify critical limitations consistent with recent findings: evidential uncertainty decomposition fails to properly separate epistemic and aleatoric components (both decrease with training data, violating theoretical expectations), and out-of-distribution detection performance is marginally above random (AUROC $\sim$ 0.55). These findings indicate that while evidential methods excel at in-distribution calibration and computational efficiency, ensemble methods remain necessary for applications requiring robust epistemic uncertainty quantification or out-of-distribution detection. Our work provides practical guidance for method selection based on application requirements, computational constraints, and the relative importance of calibration versus uncertainty decomposition.

\end{abstract}
